# Pre-Industrial Settlement Resource Reference
### Geology, biome, and climate as the foundation of local production, trade need, and growth ceiling

---

## How to use this document

For any settlement, start from three environmental inputs:

1. **Bedrock/geology** → determines ores, stone, clay, gems, minerals
2. **Biome/climate** → determines flora, fauna, fiber, fuel, husbandry
3. **Hydrology** (rivers, coast, wetland, aridity) → determines access, transport cost, and which of the above are actually reachable

Cross-reference these three and you get a settlement's **native resource base**. Anything a settlement needs but doesn't have natively becomes an **import dependency** — and the size/reliability of that dependency drives trade route design, settlement placement, and growth ceiling.

---

## 1. Metals & Ores

| Resource | Geological signal | Terrain/biome tie-in | Trade implication if absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bog iron | Iron-rich groundwater in wetlands/peat | Marsh, fen, slow rivers | Iron must be imported as billet/bloom, or tools imported finished |
| Vein/hematite iron | Iron-bearing sedimentary or metamorphic rock | Any, often hill country | Same as above — settlements without either source are structurally import-dependent for tools/weapons |
| Native/carbonate copper | Volcanic-basaltic zones; malachite/azurite (visible green-blue staining) | Often coincides with volcanic or rift terrain | Bronze-age culture must import copper or finished bronze |
| Tin | Rare granite-hosted veins | Mountainous granite belts only | Extremely common import need — historically the single biggest driver of long-distance Bronze Age trade |
| Lead (galena) | Hydrothermal veins in limestone/dolomite | Often paired with silver, sometimes zinc | Loses plumbing, pewter, glazes, pigments, net-weights, solder |
| Silver | Byproduct of argentiferous galena (cupellation) | Same terrain as lead | Currency/luxury trade good absent — economy leans barter or alternate specie (salt, cattle, textiles) |
| Gold | Alluvial (placer) in rivers draining old mountainous terrain | Rivers below granite/quartz-vein highlands | Luxury/prestige-good deficit; currency substitute needed |
| Coal (rare pre-industrial use) | Surface outcrops, sedimentary basins | Occasional early use (China, Roman Britain) | Not a hard dependency pre-industrially; charcoal substitutes |

## 2. Stone, Clay & Building Materials

| Resource | Geological signal | Downstream industry | Trade implication if absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limestone | Sedimentary basins, karst terrain | Lime mortar, plaster, early cement | Must import mortar materials or rely on timber/mud construction |
| Granite/basalt | Igneous terrain | Durable but hard-to-work stone | Slower monumental architecture; heavier labor investment |
| Flint/chert | Chalk or limestone nodules | Tools, fire-starting (pre- and para-metal) | Tool edge quality drops or must import worked flint |
| Obsidian | Volcanic glass, specific volcanic terrain | Superior cutting edges pre-metal | Strong luxury/tool trade good where present; absence pushes toward bone/flint tool reliance |
| Common clay | Riverine, floodplain, lake margin (near-universal) | Basic pottery, brick, daub | Rare true absence (glaciated bedrock, pure sand/coral zones) — settlement likely imports vessels or uses skin/wood/basket containers |
| Kaolin (white clay) | Weathered granite/feldspar | Fine ware, later porcelain | Loses luxury ceramic export potential |

## 3. Gemstones & Ornamental Minerals

| Resource | Geological signal | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Diamond | Kimberlite pipes | Extremely localized — a single-source luxury good, strong trade/conflict hook |
| Emerald, aquamarine, tourmaline, topaz | Pegmatite veins (coarse granite intrusion) | Often co-occurs with tin/tantalum-bearing terrain |
| Ruby, sapphire, garnet | Metamorphic terrain (schist, marble contact zones) | Corundum forms under high heat/pressure |
| Agate, amethyst, quartz-family | Volcanic gas cavities (geodes) | Surface-collectible — accessible without deep mining tech |
| Turquoise | Copper-bearing arid rock | Overlaps geographically with copper-producing arid regions |
| Amber | Baltic-type coastal/lignite deposits | Not mined — beachcombed or dug from soft soil; no mining tech required |
| Jade (nephrite/jadeite) | Serpentinite-associated metamorphic belts | Hard to work without specialized abrasives — presence signals high labor investment or long lapidary tradition |

## 4. Other Industrial Minerals

| Resource | Geological signal | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Sulfur | Volcanic/hot spring/fumarole zones | Pyrotechnics, medicine, bleaching/dyeing |
| Mica | Metamorphic/pegmatite terrain | Pre-glass window substitute |
| Ochre/hematite | Near-universal (surface iron oxide weathering) | Pigment — viable even at zero metallurgy |
| Alum | Volcanic/sedimentary evaporite | Dye mordant, tanning — absence creates real textile-dye trade dependency |
| Salt | Evaporite basins, salt domes, brine springs, or coastal solar evaporation | Preservation, diet, currency-equivalent — near-universal need, unevenly distributed source |

## 5. Flora-Derived Resources

| Resource | Biome signal | Product | Import need if absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hardwood forest (oak, ash) | Temperate | Shipbuilding, tools, charcoal | Weak construction/shipbuilding capacity; charcoal scarcity limits metalwork |
| Conifer forest | Temperate/boreal | Pitch, tar, resin, turpentine | Loses waterproofing/sealant industry (critical for ships, barrels) |
| Flax/hemp | Temperate, needs standing water for retting | Linen, rope, sailcloth | Textile and cordage imports needed |
| Cotton | Warm/semi-arid with irrigation | Cotton textiles | N/A outside suitable climate band — structural, not solvable locally |
| Wool-fiber pasture | Open grazing land | Wool textiles | Requires pastoral neighbor/trade partner |
| Dye plants (madder, woad, indigo) | Specific temperate/subtropical zones | Dye | Textiles stay undyed or muted; dye becomes luxury import |
| Grape-viable climate | Mediterranean-type (warm, dry summer) | Wine | Beer/mead substitute, or wine import |
| Grain surplus | Arable temperate/subtropical | Beer, bread, storable staple | Core food security question, not just industry |
| Bee-forage flowering land | Temperate/subtropical | Honey, beeswax (candles, lost-wax casting, waterproofing) | Loses sweetener and casting-wax industry |

## 6. Fauna-Derived Resources & Husbandry

| Terrain/biome | Domesticate | Economy shape |
|---|---|---|
| Grassland/steppe | Cattle, horses, sheep | Dairy, wool, leather, large-scale pastoralism, mobility/military advantage (horses) |
| Mountainous/rugged | Goats (browsers, poor-forage tolerant) | Dairy and fiber (mohair/cashmere-type), less meat/grain surplus |
| Forest/woodland edge | Pigs (mast foragers) | Minimal infrastructure meat economy |
| Arid/desert | Camels/camelids | Long-distance caravan trade, water-efficient dairy/fiber |
| Riverine/wetland | Water buffalo–type, waterfowl | Flood-plain agriculture-adjacent economy |
| Island/isolated ecosystem | Often none viable | Human-labor-intensive substitute economy; strong trade dependency for draft power and leather |

Note: presence of huntable game does not guarantee domestication — temperament, social hierarchy, and captive breeding viability gate it (real-world zebra-vs-horse problem). A society can sit in a game-rich environment and still lack livestock, which is a legitimate and useful worldbuilding lever.

## 7. Base Craft Industries (near-universal, with real absence conditions)

| Industry | Requires | Realistic absence condition |
|---|---|---|
| Pottery | Workable clay + fuel | Glaciated bedrock, pure sand/coral terrain |
| Basketry/cordage | Reeds, willow, vines, bark fiber | Desert, tundra (sinew/hide cordage substitutes) |
| Tanning/leather | Any husbandry/hunting + tannin source (bark) or alum | Low-tannin, non-alum region forces smoke/brain tanning (lower quality) |
| Fermentation (beer/wine/mead) | Storable sugar/starch surplus | Chronic food scarcity or no storage tech |
| Woodworking | Forest access, hardwood for fine work | Treeless plain/steppe/desert — imports furniture/ships or substitutes hide/bone construction |
| Glazed ceramics | Access to lead, ash/silica, or tin oxide | No mineral glaze source — stays at low-fired unglazed ware |

## 8. Composite Settlement Profiles

Combining bedrock + biome + hydrology gives coherent "packages" — useful as generation templates:

- **Wetland + iron-bearing clay** → bog-iron smithing culture; likely exports tools/blades, imports whatever the wetland can't grow (grain, timber)
- **Arid basin, no forest** → mudbrick architecture, salt/textile economy; structurally imports timber and metal, exports salt/dyed cloth
- **Volcanic coastal + obsidian** → strong pre-metal tool tradition; may delay metallurgy adoption since obsidian is "good enough" locally
- **Mountain granite + co-located tin/copper** → strong bronze-age hub, likely trade nexus since tin+copper co-occurrence is rare
- **Grassland/steppe** → pastoral/mobile economy; exports livestock, wool, leather; imports grain, metal tools, pottery from settled neighbors
- **River floodplain, temperate** → default "generalist" settlement: pottery, grain, some fiber, timber nearby; import needs are narrower and more luxury-skewed (metals, gems, dyes) rather than subsistence-critical

## 9. Reading this as a trade-need generator

For any settlement, run the checklist:

1. **Metals**: iron / copper / tin / lead-silver / gold — present, partial, or absent?
2. **Salt**: local source or import?
3. **Fiber/textile base**: which fiber (flax, wool, cotton) is native, and what's the dye situation?
4. **Fuel**: forest for charcoal, peat, or scarcity forcing dung/import?
5. **Husbandry**: full livestock suite, partial (e.g., goats but no cattle), or none — draft power and leather implications
6. **Ceramics**: clay quality (coarse vs. kaolin) and glaze mineral access
7. **Luxury/prestige goods**: gems, amber, fine dye, gold — what does this settlement have that's genuinely rare, and what does it lack that a wealthy neighbor would?

Whatever comes up short in this checklist is a standing import dependency — and the size of that gap, weighed against the settlement's exportable surplus, is what determines whether it's a trade hub, a periphery client, or a self-sufficient backwater. Distance and terrain to the nearest source of each missing input then set the actual trade route logic.

## 10. Quantitative Anchors for Simulation

The sections above establish *what* a settlement can plausibly produce. This section gives *how much* — real figures pulled from geochemistry, archaeometallurgy, and agricultural-history research, usable directly as rate constants, ratios, or per-hectare/per-capita multipliers in a simulation.

### 10.1 Ore scarcity — crustal abundance (sets relative rarity/discovery odds)

Elemental crustal abundance is a reasonable proxy for how hard a given metal is to find, independent of ore-body concentration:

| Element | Crustal abundance | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | ~50,000 ppm (~5%) | Tin sources and trade during antiquity (Wikipedia, citing standard geochemical abundance tables) |
| Copper | ~70 ppm | same |
| Lead | ~16 ppm | same |
| Tin | ~2 ppm | same |
| Silver | ~0.1 ppm | same |
| Gold | ~0.005 ppm (5 ppb) | same |

Usable directly as a relative-rarity weighting: iron is roughly **700× more abundant** than copper, and copper roughly **35× more abundant** than tin — which is the quantitative reason tin, not copper, was the Bronze Age's chronic long-distance trade good. If you want a "does this tile have an ore deposit" roll, weighting deposit-spawn probability by (roughly) the inverse of these ratios reproduces the real historical scarcity gradient reasonably well. Note crustal abundance ≠ ore-body abundance — economically workable deposits are far rarer and geographically clustered (hydrothermal veins, pegmatites, etc.), so treat these ratios as the *floor* of scarcity, not the deposit-frequency itself.

### 10.2 Bloomery iron smelting — fuel and yield ratios

From archaeometallurgical reconstruction studies and experimental archaeology:

- **Ore-to-bloom**: roughly 41 kg of ore → 13.5 kg of usable bloom iron in one well-documented reconstruction (≈33% recovery by mass) — figures reported for Southern Levant bloomery reconstructions.
- **Charcoal-to-bloom**: the same reconstruction used 91 kg of charcoal for 13.5 kg of iron — a **charcoal:iron mass ratio of roughly 6.7:1**.
- **Charge ratio during smelting**: experimental smelts commonly run a charcoal:ore ratio near **1:1 to 3:1 by volume**, adjusted for how reducing an atmosphere the smelter wants.
- **Furnace scale over time**: early European bloomeries yielded under 1 kg of iron per firing; by the late 14th century, average bloom size had risen to roughly 15 kg per firing, and water-powered bloomeries eventually reached blooms of around 300 kg.
- **Smelt duration**: a single bloomery run in reconstructions takes roughly **6–10 hours of continuous operation** with constant bellows attention.

Simulation use: a per-firing labor/fuel cost function of `charcoal_kg ≈ 6.7 × iron_kg_out` plus a multi-hour continuous-labor requirement is a defensible baseline for a pre-1300s bloomery; scale the multiplier down and batch size up for late-medieval water-powered furnaces.

### 10.3 Charcoal supply — sustainable yield per hectare of managed woodland

From fuel-consumption reconstructions of ancient/medieval iron production (comparative studies from Elba Island and East African case studies):

- A highly managed, fast-growing coppice can sustain roughly **4,000 kg of charcoal per hectare per year** at maximum yield.
- A more conservative, realistic sustained yield is closer to **1,000 kg of charcoal per hectare per year**.
- High-quality metallurgical charcoal conversion efficiency from wood is estimated at roughly **0.05–0.10 kg of charcoal per kg of wood** in some reconstructions (i.e., a 10–20:1 wood-to-charcoal ratio by this estimate) — other sources report a less severe ~4-5:1 wood-to-charcoal ratio, so treat this as a wide-uncertainty band depending on kiln technology and wood species.
- A documented real-world consequence: on resource-constrained islands (Elba), simulation studies estimate a non-trivial chance (modeled around 5–14% of yearly outcomes) that available woodland was insufficient to meet smelting fuel demand, which historically forced ore transport to the mainland instead of fuel transport to the ore.

Simulation use: this gives you a direct land-area constraint — `hectares_of_managed_coppice_needed = annual_charcoal_demand_kg / 1000` (conservative) — and a legitimate failure mode: a settlement can be ore-rich but fuel-poor, and the realistic response (attested archaeologically) is moving ore to fuel, not fuel to ore, when transport favors it (e.g., coastal/sea access).

### 10.4 Grain agriculture — yield ratios and per-hectare output

From medieval English manorial account databases (tens of thousands of yield observations) and empirical reconstruction trials:

- **Seed-to-yield ratio**: medieval European grain yields commonly returned **1:3 to 1:4** (three to four grains harvested per grain sown), with some regions/years as low as 1:3 being treated as close to subsistence floor. A minimum ratio of roughly **1:3** is generally treated as the floor needed to sustain a population at all, since one of every three grains must be reserved as next year's seed.
- **Absolute yield**: long-run cereal yields in medieval England are estimated at roughly **500 kg/hectare**, versus ~2,000 kg/ha after the Industrial Revolution and ~8,000 kg/ha in the post-Green-Revolution period — a useful anchor if you want tech-tier multipliers on farm output (medieval ≈ 1×, early industrial ≈ 4×, modern ≈ 16×).
- **Seed input rate**: reconstruction trials at a medieval site used roughly **60 kg of seed grain per hectare**, which at a 1:3 return gives about 180 kg/ha — broadly consistent with the ~500 kg/ha figure once better soils/years are averaged in.

Simulation use: `harvest_kg_per_hectare ≈ seed_rate_kg_per_hectare × yield_ratio`, with yield_ratio drawn from a distribution centered on 3–4 for a baseline medieval tech tier, and a hard floor at 3 below which the population is in a structural food deficit (all seed grain consumed, no reserve for next planting).

### 10.5 Livestock — stocking density and animal-unit equivalence

Historical/agronomic stocking-rate literature gives a standardized way to compare mixed-species herds and translate pasture area into supportable livestock (and thus into wool/dairy/leather/meat output). This is modern-agronomy sourced, so treat absolute numbers as an upper bound and scale down for unimproved pre-industrial pasture:

- Standard unit: the **Dry Sheep Equivalent (DSE)** or **Animal Unit (AU)**, typically anchored to one ~1,000 lb (450 kg) steer eating about 3% of its body weight per day.
- Rough equivalences: 1 dry goat ≈ 1 DSE; 1 yearling steer ≈ 8 DSE; 1 lactating cow ≈ up to 25 DSE.
- Regulatory extensification benchmarks (useful as a sustainable-pasture ceiling) have used roughly **1.4 livestock units per hectare** as an upper bound before overgrazing risk rises sharply on temperate pasture.
- On lower-productivity/rangeland pasture (semi-arid, unimproved), carrying capacity drops an order of magnitude — real-world estimates for browse/grassland rangeland run as low as **0.3 tropical-livestock-units per hectare per year** (i.e., roughly 3 hectares needed to support one large-animal-equivalent).

Simulation use: give improved temperate pasture a ceiling near 1–1.5 AU/hectare and unimproved/marginal rangeland a ceiling closer to 0.2–0.3 AU/hectare — a roughly 5–7× difference in land needed per animal — and derive wool/dairy/leather/meat output as a function of supported AU count rather than raw pasture hectares.

### 10.6 Salt production — pre-industrial evaporation yield

Direct pre-industrial production-rate figures are scarce (research explicitly notes few prior attempts to quantify pre-industrial evaporation output), but the underlying physical methodology has been formalized:

- Academic reconstructions of ancient/pre-industrial salt-making quantify three production pathways — direct solar evaporation, externally heated brine, and immersed heated-stone evaporation — each with different fuel and time costs. Solar evaporation requires no fuel input but is climate-gated (needs an evaporation rate that exceeds precipitation, i.e., warm/dry climate with steady wind); heated-brine methods work in any climate but carry a real fuel (wood/peat) cost per unit of salt.
- As an order-of-magnitude anchor from early industrial solar salt-works (pre-mechanized, comparable to late pre-industrial technique): the U.S. Atlantic seaboard produced on the order of **half a million bushels of solar sea salt per year** around 1830 using open-pond evaporation — useful mainly as a sense of scale for a large, dedicated coastal salt-works rather than a per-hectare constant.

Simulation use: model salt production as two distinct pathways with different input costs — solar (climate-gated, ~zero fuel cost, slow) versus boiled brine (climate-independent, real fuel-per-unit-output cost, faster) — rather than a single uniform rate; this reproduces why some pre-industrial cultures specialized in coastal solar salt while inland/northern cultures relied on fuel-intensive brine-boiling instead.

### 10.7 Population density by subsistence mode (sets settlement-size ceiling per land-use type)

This is probably the single most useful simulation anchor, since it converts land-use type directly into supportable population — a widely-cited comparative table (Pingali, by way of secondary summary) gives:

| Subsistence mode | People supported per km² |
|---|---|
| Gathering / forest fallow | 0–4 |
| Bush fallow | 4–64 |
| Short fallow (hoe + animal traction) | 14–64 |
| Annual cultivation | 64–256 |

Separately, ethnographic hunter-gatherer density studies report a very wide real-world range — from about **0.15 people/km²** (Kalahari-type arid foraging) up to roughly **10 people/km²** in unusually rich/complex hunter-gatherer contexts (resource-dense coastal or riverine settings) — and one Neolithic agricultural case study (LBK settlements, ~5250–5050 BC) estimated a *local* carrying capacity as high as **8.5 people/km²**, while noting that realized population is typically only **25–70% of theoretical local carrying capacity** even in well-documented cases, and that a global (non-local) estimate for the same culture was far lower, around 0.6 people/km².

Simulation use: this gives a clean, tiered lookup — `population_ceiling = land_area_km² × density_for_subsistence_mode`, with density increasing roughly two orders of magnitude from pure foraging to intensive annual cultivation. Applying the 25–70% realized-vs-theoretical discount by default (rather than assuming settlements sit at the hard ceiling) will produce more historically realistic settlement sizes and leaves headroom for population growth/famine dynamics rather than a hard wall.

## 11. Notes on source quality

These figures come from a mix of primary geochemical datasets (USGS, peer-reviewed abundance compilations), experimental archaeology (reconstructed bloomery smelts, reconstructed medieval field trials), and long-run economic-history databases (English manorial accounts). They're good enough for internally consistent simulation rules but shouldn't be read as precise historical constants — real pre-industrial output varied heavily by regional ore grade, soil quality, climate year-to-year, and technique, often by a factor of 2–5× within the ranges cited above. Where a range is given, treat the low end as a poor/struggling settlement and the high end as a well-established, well-resourced one, and consider sampling from the range (rather than hard-coding the midpoint) if you want run-to-run variation in the simulator.
